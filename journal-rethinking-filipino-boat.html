Rethinking the traditional Filipino boat

The bangka has carried Filipino families, catch and visitors for generations. The hull has barely changed in that time. The engine strapped to it, however, is a modern problem the boat was never really designed around.

The bangka today, and what
hasn't changed

The traditional bangka is an outrigger boat: light, stable, and shallow enough to beach almost anywhere along the coast. Most working bangkas today are motorised, usually with small petrol or diesel engines.

The scale is significant. The Bureau of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources recorded 375,995 registered municipal fishing vessels in 2022 (BFAR, Philippine Fisheries Profile 2022), and well over half of the registered municipal fleet is motorised (BFAR, 2017). The hull is familiar; the engine is loud, thirsty and increasingly hard to maintain in small coastal towns.

Swapping the engine,
not the boat

ESMI's starting point is simple. Owners trust the shape of the bangka they already have. What they don't trust is the fuel bill.

Our plug-and-play solar-powered boat engines are designed to replace diesel and petrol engines on existing hulls, without redesigning the boat around them. The owner keeps the bangka they know; the propulsion becomes electric, and the running costs come down with it.

. early morning on the water .

A modular,
expandable design

Alongside the conversion engines, ESMI designs modular solar-electric boats shipped as parts rather than finished hulls. The deck can be expanded in length and width to suit the work it does: fishing, ferrying passengers, resort transfers or island-hopping. Modules are delivered where the boat is needed and assembled on site, and when one part wears out, that single part is replaced rather than the whole vessel.

The intention is to keep boats useful for longer, with fewer long trips to a distant boatyard and less time out of the water.

Costs, reefs
and tourism

Fuel is the largest recurring expense for most motorised bangka owners. An electric drive cuts that bill and removes the daily trip to the fuel station, which matters most on small islands where fuel is dearest.

The second gain is quiet. Research in Nature Communications has shown that motorboat noise roughly doubles predation on juvenile coral-reef fish (Simpson et al., Nature Communications, 2016), and a later field study found that limiting motorboat traffic measurably improves fish survival and reproduction on reefs (Nature Communications, 2022).

ESMI is not alone in asking these questions. In 2025, students at the University of the Philippines Diliman presented an electric propulsion prototype tested on a bangka in Mabini, Batangas (UP Diliman, March 2025).
